## 1. Beginner

### 1.1 Human Decision Making
- **Nudge Theory**
  - Nudge Theory suggests that people make decisions based on cognitive biases rather than purely rational analysis. It proposes that subtle changes in the way choices are presented can significantly influence people's behavior without restricting their freedom of choice.
- **Persuasive Technology**
  - Persuasive technology refers to digital products deliberately designed to change a user's attitude or behavior through interaction, not through argument or coercion. Fitness apps that reward streaks or apps that use progress bars to keep users engaged are common examples.
- **Behavior Design**
  - Behavior Design is an approach that combines elements of psychology, neuroscience, and design principles to understand and influence human behaviors. The goal behind behavior design is to make it easier for users to accomplish their goals or desired actions within a product, service, or system.
- **Behavioral Science**
  - Behavioral science is the interdisciplinary study of human behavior, which encompasses disciplines like psychology, sociology, and anthropology. This field- primarily focuses on understanding what impacts our decisions, actions, and emotions.
- **Behavioral Economics**
  - Behavioral Economics is a subfield of economics that studies the psychological, social, and emotional factors that influence decision-making and economic behavior. It seeks to understand why people make choices that deviate from the traditional economic model, which assumes that individuals behave rationally and seek to maximize their utility.
- **BJ Fogg's Behavior Model**
  - B.J. Fogg, a renowned psychologist, and researcher at Stanford University, proposed the Fogg Behavior Model (FBM).
- **CREATE Action Funnel**
  - The CREATE Action Funnel breaks the path to a user action into six stages: Cue, Reaction, Evaluation, Ability, Timing, and Execution. A user must pass through each stage for the target behavior to happen, and a failure at any stage stops the action.
- **Spectrum of Thinking Interventions**
  - The spectrum of thinking interventions ranges from automatic, low-effort nudges on one end to interventions that demand deliberate, conscious thought on the other. Design choices sit somewhere along this spectrum depending on how much reflection the target behavior needs.
- **Dual Process Theory**
  - Dual process theory describes two systems of thinking: a fast, automatic, intuitive system and a slower, effortful, analytical system. Most daily decisions rely on the fast system, which is why habits and defaults are so powerful in shaping behavior.

### 1.2 Behavior Change Strategies
- **BJ Fogg's Behavior Grid**
  - The BJ Fogg Behavior Grid is a framework that helps UX designers, product managers, and marketers understand and identify different types of behavior change. Created by Stanford University professor B.J.

### 1.3 Cheating
- **Defaulting**
  - Defaulting means setting a pre-selected option so the user has to opt out rather than opt in. Because most people stick with whatever is already selected, this single design choice can dramatically shift outcomes, such as organ donation rates or retirement plan enrollment.
- **Making it Incidental**
  - Making an action incidental means folding it into a task the user is already doing, so it happens as a side effect rather than a separate decision. An example is a payment app that automatically rounds up purchases and saves the difference, so saving money requires no extra effort from the user.
- **Automate the Act of Repetition**
  - Automating repetition removes the user from having to repeat an action manually once it has been done or approved once. Recurring subscriptions, auto-renewals, and scheduled backups are examples where the system takes over a task after the first setup.

## 2. Intermediate

### 2.1 Make or Change Habits
- **Help user Avoiding the Cue**
  - Helping a user avoid the cue means removing or hiding the trigger that sets off an unwanted habit. If a notification prompts compulsive checking, muting or removing that notification eliminates the starting point of the loop.

### 2.2 Support Conscious Action
- **Educate & Encourage User**
  - Educating and encouraging the user means providing clear information about a choice along with positive reinforcement to help them decide with confidence. This can take the form of tooltips, explainer content, or contextual tips shown at the moment a decision is being made.
- **Help User think about their Action**
  - This design approach slows the user down at a key decision point so they consciously weigh the consequences of an action before confirming it. Confirmation screens, cooling-off periods, and summary reviews before a purchase are common examples.
- **Replace the Routine**
  - Replacing the routine means keeping the same cue and reward but substituting a different action in between. For example, if boredom triggers snacking, a product might prompt a short walk instead while still delivering a comparable reward like distraction or a mood boost.
- **Use Consciousness to Interfere**
  - Using consciousness to interfere means inserting a moment of deliberate awareness into an otherwise automatic habit loop. A confirmation dialog before a purchase or a screen time warning before opening an app forces the user out of autopilot.
- **Mindfulness to Avoid Acting on the Cue**
  - This technique trains users to notice a habit cue as it happens and choose not to act on it, rather than removing the cue itself. Apps that prompt a brief pause or breathing exercise when a craving cue appears use this approach.
- **Crowd Out Old Habit with New Behavior**
  - Crowding out an old habit means introducing a new, incompatible behavior that occupies the same cue and reward slot as the unwanted one. If checking a phone at bedtime is the target, replacing it with reading a book at that same moment can push the old habit out over time.

### 2.3 Understanding the Product
- **Target Outcome**
  - The target outcome is the result the business or user gets once the target action is completed, such as increased retention, revenue, or user satisfaction. It connects the user's action back to a measurable goal that justifies the design effort.
- **Target Actor**
  - The target actor is the type of user the product is designed for, defined by their goals, context, and constraints rather than just demographics. Clarifying the target actor early prevents a design from trying to serve everyone and satisfying no one well.
- **Target Action**
  - The target action is the specific behavior the product is designed to get users to perform, such as completing a purchase, submitting a form, or returning the next day. Defining it clearly early in a project keeps design decisions focused on a measurable outcome.
- **Business Model Canvas**
  - The Business Model Canvas is a strategic management and visual representation tool that allows you to describe, design, challenge, and pivot your existing business model. Developed by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, it helps organizations to understand how they create, deliver, and capture value.

### 2.4 Conceptual Design
- **Nir Eyal's Hook Model**
  - Nir Eyal's Hook Model describes a four-step loop that products use to build habitual engagement: trigger, action, variable reward, and investment. The trigger prompts the user, the action is the simplest behavior done in anticipation of a reward, the reward keeps the user interested through variability, and the investment step has the user put something into the product that makes future triggers more effective.
- **Cue Routine Reward Model**
  - The Cue-Routine-Reward model explains a habit as a loop where a cue triggers a routine, and that routine produces a reward that reinforces the cue-routine connection. Over repeated cycles, the routine becomes automatic once the cue appears, requiring less conscious thought each time.
- **Create User Personas**
  - Creating user personas means building fictional but research-based profiles that represent the key user types a product serves. Each persona typically includes goals, frustrations, behaviors, and context drawn from interviews, surveys, or analytics rather than guesswork.
- **Lean Canvas**
  - Lean Canvas is a variation of the Business Model Canvas adapted for early-stage startups, replacing some blocks with problem, solution, and key metrics sections. It puts more focus on the problem being solved and the unfair advantage a startup has over competitors.
- **Business Model Inspirator**
  - A Business Model Inspirator is a tool or method that helps you to generate new or creative ideas for the strategic, operational, and financial aspects of a business. It helps entrepreneurs, startups, and established companies to explore different ways of designing or improving their business models by drawing inspiration from various sources.
- **Competitor Analysis**
  - Competitor analysis is the process of researching other products or businesses that solve a similar problem for similar users. It covers their features, pricing, positioning, and user experience to find gaps and opportunities for differentiation.
- **User Stories**
  - A user story is a short description of a feature written from the perspective of the user, typically following the format "as a \[user\], I want \[goal\] so that \[benefit\]." It keeps the product backlog focused on user needs rather than technical implementation details. Teams use user stories to prioritize what to build and to write acceptance criteria for each feature.
- **In general, keep it short and simple**
  - This principle says that any task or flow presented to the user should be reduced to the fewest possible steps and the simplest possible language. Extra steps, fields, or explanations increase the chance a user gives up before completing the task.
- **Make it easy to understand and complete**
  - This principle focuses on making sure users know what a task requires and can finish it without confusion or friction. Clear instructions, sensible input formats, and forgiving validation all support this.
- **Make progress visible to user**
  - Making progress visible means showing users where they are in a multi-step process, such as through a progress bar, step counter, or checklist. It reduces uncertainty and helps users judge how much effort remains before they reach completion.
- **Figma**
  - Figma is a powerful and versatile web-based design tool that allows designers, developers, and stakeholders to collaborate on UI and UX projects in real-time. It's an excellent choice for creating wireframes and high-fidelity prototypes and supports vector editing, responsive design, and team collaboration.
- **Sketch**
  - Sketch is a powerful digital design tool specifically tailored for user interface (UI) and user experience (UX) design. As part of the creative process, designers use Sketch to create wireframes, visual mockups, and interactive prototypes that help plan and iterate their ideas.

## 3. Advanced

### 3.1 Prototyping
- **Five Forces Model**
  - The Five Forces Model, developed by Michael Porter, analyzes an industry's competitiveness through five factors: existing competition, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, and the bargaining power of suppliers and buyers. It helps teams understand how much pressure a business faces from its market environment beyond just direct competitors.
- **SWOT Analysis**
  - SWOT analysis evaluates a business or product across four categories: strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. Strengths and weaknesses look inward at the organization itself, while opportunities and threats look outward at the market and competition.
- **Make progress meaningful to reward user**
  - This principle says that visible progress should feel like a real reward, not just a mechanical status update. Small celebratory moments, encouraging messages, or visual milestones at meaningful points keep users motivated to continue.
- **Make successful completion clearly visible**
  - This principle emphasizes giving users an unmistakable signal when they have finished a task successfully, such as a confirmation screen, success message, or visual change. Ambiguous endings leave users unsure whether their action worked, which can lead to repeated submissions or abandoned trust in the product.
- **Customer Experience Map by Mel Edwards**
  - The Customer Experience Map is a deliverable developed by Mel Edwards that visualizes a user's journey through a product across stages, capturing their actions, thoughts, and emotions at each point. It highlights pain points and moments of friction alongside points of delight across the full experience.
- **Simple Flowchart**
  - A simple flowchart is a diagram that maps out the steps and decision points in a process using basic shapes like boxes and arrows. It shows the logical path a user or system takes from a starting point to an outcome, including any branches.
- **Event-driven Process Chain Model (EPC)**
  - The Event-driven Process Chain model, or EPC, is a diagramming notation that represents a business process as an alternating sequence of events and functions. Each function is triggered by an event and produces one or more resulting events, which can branch using logical connectors.
- **Business Process Model & Notation (BPMN)**
  - BPMN is a standardized graphical notation for modeling business processes using elements like events, activities, gateways, and swimlanes. It is more formal and detailed than a simple flowchart, making it suited for complex processes involving multiple actors or systems.
- **Good Layout Rules**
  - Good layout rules are the visual design principles that guide how elements are arranged on a screen, covering alignment, spacing, hierarchy, and grouping. Following them makes an interface easier to scan and reduces the mental effort needed to find information.
- **Balsamiq**
  - Balsamiq is a popular wireframing tool that helps designers, developers, and product managers to quickly create and visualize user interfaces, web pages, or app screens. It's an easy-to-use software that allows you to focus on ideas and concepts rather than getting caught up in pixel-perfect designs.
- **Call to Action**
  - A Call to Action (CTA) is a UX pattern that encourages users to take a specific action on a website, app, or other digital product. It is an essential component of effective UX design, as it helps guide users through the experience and accomplish their goals.
- **Status Reports**
  - Status reports are UI elements that summarize the current state of a process, account, or system for the user, such as a dashboard showing recent activity or an order tracking screen. They let users quickly check where things stand without digging through details.
- **How-to-Tips**
  - How-to-tips are short pieces of guidance shown to users at the moment they need help completing a specific action. They appear as tooltips, inline hints, or brief instructions rather than long documentation.

### 3.2 UX Best Practices
- **Reminders & Planning Prompts**
  - Reminders and planning prompts are notifications or in-app messages that nudge a user to complete a pending task or plan an upcoming action. They work by re-capturing attention at a relevant moment rather than requiring the user to remember on their own.
- **Decision-Making Support**
  - Decision making support plays a crucial role in UX design by providing users with the necessary information, options, and feedback to make informed choices when interacting with a product or interface. This UX pattern focuses on helping users make decisions through clear presentation of information, minimizing cognitive load, and offering guidance throughout the various interactions.
- **Behavior Change Games**
  - Behavior change games are a powerful UX design pattern that help users adopt new habits or make positive lifestyle changes. These games are typically designed to be engaging, enjoyable, and motivating, utilizing various game elements and mechanics to encourage users to take desired actions.
- **Gamification**
  - Gamification is a UX design pattern that involves incorporating elements of game design into non-game applications or websites to enhance user engagement and create enjoyable experiences. By using game mechanics like points, achievements, competition, and progress-tracking, designers can make mundane tasks more appealing and motivate users to interact with the content or perform certain actions.
- **Reminders**
  - Reminders play an essential role in the user's experience, especially when it comes to retaining user attention and engagement. They can be helpful in making sure that the user stays focused on their tasks, remembers essential information or deadlines, and continually interacts with your product or design.
- **Social Sharing**
  - In the realm of UX design, Social Sharing is a crucial pattern that allows users to share content from a website or app to various social media platforms. It helps in creating interaction between users and the content, engaging the audience and generating more traffic to the source.
- **Goal Trackers**
  - Goal trackers are essential UX patterns that enable users to monitor their progress towards accomplishing a specific task or objective. Essentially, they help users stay motivated, organized and informed about their journey within an app, website, or system.
- **Clear the Page of Distractions**
  - Clearing the page of distractions means removing any element that competes with the user's attention on the target action, such as unrelated links, ads, or excessive navigation. A cleaner page increases the odds a user notices and completes the intended action.
- **Make it Clear, Where to Act**
  - This practice focuses on making the actionable element, such as a button or link, visually obvious through size, color, contrast, or placement. If users cannot quickly locate where to act, they hesitate or abandon the task even if they understand what to do.
- **Tell User what the Action is and ask for it**
  - This practice means stating plainly what the user should do next instead of leaving them to infer it from vague copy or an unclear button. Direct, specific requests such as "Add to cart" outperform vague ones like "Learn more" when the goal is a specific action.

### 3.3 Measuring the Impact
- **Planners**
  - Planners are an essential aspect of UX design, helping users accomplish complex tasks by breaking them down into manageable steps. Planners are particularly useful in situations where users are expected to make decisions or deal with a range of options.
- **Tutorials**
  - Tutorials are an important aspect of UX Design, as they help users understand and learn how to navigate through a new application, software or website. It is essential to create engaging, informative and easy-to-follow tutorials to ensure users can quickly comprehend the interface and become efficient in navigating it.
- **Make UI Professional and Beautiful**
  - A professional and visually polished interface builds immediate trust with users, often before they read any content. Inconsistent spacing, mismatched fonts, or a dated look can make users doubt the credibility of a product, regardless of how good the underlying functionality is.
- **Deploy Social Proof**
  - Social proof shows users that other people have used, trusted, or approved of a product, through reviews, testimonials, user counts, or ratings. People tend to look to others' behavior when they are uncertain about a decision, so visible social proof reduces hesitation.
- **Deploy Strong Authority on Subject**
  - Displaying authority means showing credentials, expertise, or endorsements relevant to the subject matter, such as certifications, media mentions, or an author's professional background. Users are more likely to trust and follow guidance from a source that appears knowledgeable.
- **Be Authentic and Personal**
  - When creating a user experience (UX) design, it's essential to be authentic and personal. This means that your design should be genuine, truthful, and relatable to your users.
- **Prime User-Relevant Associations**
  - Priming means exposing users to words, images, or cues related to a desired outcome just before they make a decision, so that concept is more mentally accessible. For example, showing images of relaxation before a spa booking flow can make the decision feel more aligned with the user's goals.
- **Leverage Loss-Aversion**
  - Loss-aversion is the tendency for people to feel the pain of losing something more strongly than the pleasure of gaining something equivalent. Designers use this by framing messages around what a user stands to lose, such as "don't lose your progress" instead of "keep your progress," which tends to be more motivating.
- **Use Peer Comparisons**
  - Peer comparisons show users how their behavior or choices compare to others in a similar group, such as "you used less energy than 80% of similar households." This taps into social norms, since people tend to adjust their behavior to align with what others like them are doing. It is commonly used to encourage a favorable decision by framing the target choice as the normal one.
- **Use Competition**
  - Using competition means framing a decision or task as a contest against other users, such as leaderboards or limited spots, to motivate a favorable choice. People are often more willing to act quickly or commit fully when they sense they are competing against others.
- **Avoid Cognitive Overhead**
  - Cognitive overhead refers to the mental effort needed to understand or operate a given system, tool, or interface. In UX design, it is crucial to minimize cognitive overhead to create user-friendly and efficient experiences.
- **Avoid Choice Overload**
  - Avoiding direct payments means reducing the visible friction of parting with money, for example by using stored credit, points, or a saved card instead of asking users to re-enter payment details each time. Research shows the physical or visible act of paying creates psychological pain that can reduce purchase likelihood.
- **Avoid Direct Payments**
  - Avoiding direct payments is a crucial aspect of UX design that can lead to favorable conscious evaluations from users. Direct payments refer to instances where users are required to pay for your product or service upfront, which can create a negative perception and less willingness to engage.
- **Frame Text to Avoid Temporal Myopia**
  - Temporal myopia is the tendency for people to undervalue future consequences in favor of immediate ones. Framing text to counter this means making future benefits or costs feel more immediate and concrete, such as showing exactly how much money is lost per month rather than per year.
- **Remind of Prior Commitment to Act**
  - This technique reminds users of a commitment they already made, such as a goal they set or an item they added to a wishlist, to prompt them to follow through. People feel internal pressure to stay consistent with past commitments, which this pattern leverages to drive action.
- **Make Commitment to Friends**
  - This technique has users publicly commit to a goal or action in front of friends or their social network, increasing the social cost of not following through. Because people want to appear consistent to others, a public commitment adds pressure beyond the user's own motivation.
- **Make Reward Scarce**
  - Making a reward scarce means limiting its availability by time, quantity, or exclusivity, such as a limited-time discount or a small number of remaining spots. Scarcity increases perceived value and creates urgency to act before the opportunity disappears.
- **Elicit Implementation Intentions**
  - Eliciting implementation intentions means prompting users to specify exactly when, where, or how they will perform an intended action, rather than leaving the intention vague. Research shows that a concrete plan, such as picking a specific time to exercise, makes follow-through significantly more likely than a general intention alone.
- **Default Everything**
  - Defaulting everything means pre-filling or pre-selecting sensible choices throughout an interface so the user can proceed with minimal input, only changing what they specifically want to adjust. This reduces the effort required to complete a task, since accepting the default is always easier than making an active choice.
- **Lessen the Burden of Action / Info**
  - This practice focuses on reducing the amount of effort or information a user must provide to complete a task, such as shortening forms, auto-filling known data, or breaking a long process into smaller steps. Every additional field or requirement is a chance for a user to stop and reconsider or abandon the task.
- **Deploy Peer Comparisons**
  - Deploying peer comparisons in the context of making an action easy means showing users what similar people typically do, which can reduce hesitation by making the target action feel normal and low-risk. Seeing that others in a similar situation completed the same action reassures a user that it is a safe and reasonable choice.
- **Incremental A/B Testing**
  - Incremental A/B testing compares two versions of a design that differ by a single, small change, showing each version to a separate group of users and measuring which performs better. Because only one variable changes at a time, teams can clearly attribute any difference in outcome to that specific change.
- **Multivariate Testing**
  - Multivariate testing compares multiple design variations that differ across several elements at once, such as headline, image, and button color, to see which combination performs best. Unlike a simple test of one variable, it can reveal how different elements interact with each other.
- **Gather Lessons, Prioritize & Integrate**
  - This step involves collecting insights from testing and user data, deciding which findings matter most, and feeding them back into the product's design. Not every lesson learned is worth acting on immediately, so teams prioritize based on expected impact and effort required.
